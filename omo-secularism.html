3.5.3.3. Sri Aurobindo
3.5.3.3.1. Grok: AI tool by X (formerly Twitter)
  • Sri Aurobindo (1872–1950) viewed Western-style secularism critically. He saw it as a product of the European emphasis on outer life, reason, and material progress, divorced from deeper spiritual insight. In his analysis, this trajectory naturally leads to an “atheistic or agnostic cult of secularism.”
  • Western Secularism as a Historical Outcome
    • In The Foundations of Indian Culture (and related writings collected in the Complete Works), he traces how the Western mind prioritizes the tangible, visible, and practical. Religion was progressively sidelined: first confined, then rationalized, and finally reduced to a private, optional matter or eliminated. He wrote that modern Europe separated religion from life, philosophy, art, science, politics, and most social existence so that reason and life could stand on their own. The logical end of this process is secularism as the “acme of denial.”
    • He noted that ancient European religion had been more integrated with civic life, but the Christian interlude and subsequent reaction against ecclesiastical control accelerated the secularizing tendency.
  • Contrast with the Indian Temperament
    • Sri Aurobindo held that India does not share this opposition between reason/life and spirituality. In Indian civilization, “philosophy and religion, philosophy made dynamic by religion, religion enlightened by philosophy have led, the rest follow as best they can.” Spirituality is not an exotic addition but the soil and ground of the culture. There is no natural distinction between the “secular” and the spiritual; a deeper spiritual truth is seen as underlying every human activity—politics, business, art, science, and society.
    • Consequently, a Western model that aims at neutrality toward (or separation from) spirituality is ill-suited to India’s svadharma (own nature and law of being). Forcing such a separation would go against the country’s deepest instincts and historical genius.
  • Equality of Religions vs. Western Secularism
    • If “secularism” simply means equal respect and freedom for all religions, Aurobindo regarded this as already inherent in the Indian tradition (Sanatana Dharma). It did not require a modern political doctrine imported from Europe. Indian religious thought had long accepted multiple approaches to the Divine. He also observed that, despite its strong spiritual orientation, India historically never developed a theocracy.
    • He distinguished true religion (the spiritual seeking that lives in the Spirit and seeks to inform all parts of the being) from religionism (creeds, sects, churches, dogmatic systems, and external forms). The latter has often been a source of conflict, obstruction to science and philosophy, and political misuse. The spiritual essence alone is “supremely needful.”
  • Nationalism and the Spiritual Ideal
    • Aurobindo’s political vision was one of spiritual nationalism. He described Indian nationalism as “largely Hindu in its spirit and traditions” because of the continuity of civilization and culture created by the Hindu people, yet “wide enough also to include the Moslem and his culture and traditions and absorb them into itself.” He rejected both narrow communalism and a rootless secular nationalism that would discard India’s spiritual past. In his view, Sanatana Dharma is not merely a creed but “life itself”—something to be lived rather than merely believed.
    • He did not advocate a theocratic state or the political dominance of any sect. His larger evolutionary vision (Integral Yoga) aims at the spiritual transformation of life and society itself, moving beyond organized religion toward a higher consciousness that can uplift all human activities.
  • Summary of His Stance
    • Western secularism (separation of religion from public life, leading toward materialism or agnosticism) is a natural but limited development of the European mind and is not the right path for India.
    • India’s regeneration should draw consciously on its spiritual heritage so that spirituality informs and elevates every domain of life.
    • Equal respect for all religions is already part of the Indian tradition and does not require a Western secular framework.
    • True progress lies in advancing from religionism to genuine spirituality, not in banishing the spiritual from the public sphere.
  • These views appear across his political writings (especially from the nationalist period), The Foundations of Indian Culture, The Human Cycle, and later letters and essays. Interpreters associated with the Sri Aurobindo Ashram and related institutions consistently present him as opposing both dogmatic theocracy and a purely materialistic or “godless” secularism.
